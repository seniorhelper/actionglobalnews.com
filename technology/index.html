Technology

Technology

Software, simulation and applied AI — covered from the point of view of the businesses that have to deploy it, not the labs that announce it.

Technology coverage has a persistent bias toward announcement. A model is released, a funding round closes, a keynote happens, and the trade press moves as one. What gets covered far less is the part that determines whether any of it matters: what happens when an ordinary organisation tries to put the thing into production and discovers what it actually costs to run.

This desk is aimed at that gap. We cover applied technology from the point of view of the business that has to deploy it, maintain it, and explain it to a customer when it misbehaves. That means less enthusiasm about capability and more attention to constraint.

What we watch

Applied AI. Conversational systems, retrieval, and the emerging class of agentic software that takes actions rather than producing text. The interesting questions here are about scope, failure modes and liability, not about benchmark scores.

Search and discovery. How people find businesses is changing, and the mechanisms are genuinely different from the ones that governed the last fifteen years. Structured data, entity identity and machine-readable publishing are now load-bearing in a way they were not.

Browser capability. A large amount of software that required an installed application five years ago now runs acceptably in a tab. That shift is quieter than the AI story and arguably reshapes more products.

Simulation and spatial interfaces. Where three-dimensional interaction earns its cost, and the considerably larger set of cases where it does not.

We try to be specific about what a technology cannot do. An article that only lists capabilities is a brochure, and there are enough of those.
