Inflation hit 3.7% in September as gas jumped 9%. The hotter core number is what puts another Fed hike in play

Consumer prices rose 3.7% over the past year, led by a 9.1% monthly jump in gasoline. Core inflation also beat forecasts, lifting the odds of a second Federal Reserve rate increase on October 28.

AGN Action Global News Staff Edited by Zach Wennstedt · Markets desk · Published October 14, 2026 · 6 min read
LinkedInXFacebookEmail

Key takeaways

  • The consumer price index rose 0.6% in September and 3.7% over 12 months, up from 3.4% in August, according to the BLS.
  • Gasoline rose 9.1% in the month and accounted for more than half of the overall increase, while core inflation rose to 2.6%.
  • EIA data put U.S. on-highway diesel at $6.179 a gallon on October 12, $2.514 higher than a year earlier.
  • CME Group pricing showed a 43.7% chance of a Fed rate hike on October 28 after the report, up from about 20%.

Consumer prices rose 0.6% in September and 3.7% from a year earlier, the Bureau of Labor Statistics reported Wednesday, October 14, 2026. Gasoline did most of the damage: the gasoline index jumped 9.1% in a single month and accounted for more than half of the overall increase. The annual rate climbed from 3.4% in August and came in a tenth of a point above what economists had expected, according to Yahoo Finance's coverage of the release.

The part of the report that moved markets was not the pump price. Core inflation, which strips out food and energy, rose 0.3% for the month and 2.6% over the year, up from 2.4% in August. That is the measure Federal Reserve officials watch for signs that a price shock is spreading into the rest of the economy, and it came in hotter than forecasts. With the Fed's next decision due October 28, the odds of a second rate increase this fall rose sharply within hours.

What the September CPI report actually showed

The BLS release paints a picture of an economy where energy is running hot and most other categories are moving at a more ordinary pace. The energy index rose 5.2% in September and stands 20.4% above its level a year ago. Gasoline is up 34.2% from September 2025. Airline fares, which track jet fuel costs closely, rose 1.2% in the month and 22.4% over the year.

Outside of fuel, the picture is mixed rather than alarming. Grocery prices, measured as food at home, slipped 0.1% in September and are up 1.8% over twelve months. Restaurant prices, measured as food away from home, rose 0.2% and are 3.5% higher than a year ago. Shelter, the largest single component of the index, rose 0.4% in the month and 3.2% over the year. Electricity is up 4.4% year over year, and used cars and trucks are actually 1.5% cheaper than a year ago.

The monthly pattern this year has been unusually choppy. According to the BLS table, the all-items index rose 0.9% in March and 0.6% in April, fell 0.4% in June, and then climbed again with gains of 0.4% in August and 0.6% in September. Core inflation has been far steadier, mostly in a band of 0.2% to 0.4% a month.

Why gas and diesel prices are so high right now

The fuel spike is the dominant story of this inflation cycle. AAA reported that the national average for regular gasoline averaged $4.33 a gallon for September, about 50 cents above the previous September record set in 2023, according to its October 1 update. Pump prices have eased slightly since then: the U.S. Energy Information Administration's weekly survey put regular gasoline at $4.331 a gallon on October 12, down from $4.465 at the end of September.

Diesel is the bigger problem for anyone who moves goods. EIA's October 12 reading put on-highway diesel at $6.179 a gallon nationally, $2.514 higher than a year earlier. In the Rocky Mountain region, which includes Colorado, diesel averaged $6.302. Coverage of the report tied elevated diesel prices to supply disruptions linked to conflicts involving Iran and Ukraine, with transportation costs gradually feeding into the price of other goods. Fuel oil prices rose 15.3% from August, according to the same coverage.

Energy caused the headline number. The core number is what decides whether borrowing gets more expensive again this month.

What it means for Fed rate hikes and borrowing costs

The Federal Reserve raised its benchmark rate by a quarter point on September 16, to a target range of 3.75% to 4.00%, in a unanimous 12–0 vote. The policy statement said the move was aimed at a timelier return to the 2% inflation goal. That was the first increase since 2023.

After Wednesday's report, futures pricing tracked by CME Group showed a 43.7% chance of another quarter-point increase on October 28, up from roughly 20% before the release, and better-than-even odds of a second move by December. Stephen Brown of Capital Economics described the core surprise as "broad-based rather than due to any obvious temporary factors." Chicago Fed President Austan Goolsbee called the reading discouraging but said he favors waiting for more data before acting.

Bond markets reacted quickly. The 10-year Treasury yield rose above 5.3% Wednesday morning before easing to about 5.24%, and the average 30-year fixed mortgage rate tracked by Mortgage News Daily rose 10 basis points to 7.56%. The S&P 500 slipped 0.4%, the Nasdaq Composite fell 0.9% and the Dow was roughly flat.

How 3.7% inflation hits small business owners

For operators, the report carries three separate messages, and they point in slightly different directions.

  • Fuel is the cost to manage first. Any business that runs vans, trucks, delivery routes or field crews is absorbing a diesel bill roughly 70% higher than a year ago, based on EIA's national figures. Fuel surcharges, route consolidation and quoting jobs with a clear fuel-adjustment clause are the tools owners are most likely to reach for.
  • Your customers are being squeezed. Average hourly earnings rose 3% over the past year, below the 3.7% inflation rate, meaning household paychecks are losing ground. Consumer-facing businesses, especially in discretionary categories, may see shoppers trade down or delay purchases.
  • Credit may get pricier again. If the Fed raises rates on October 28, variable-rate credit lines, business credit cards and SBA loans pegged to the prime rate would adjust upward. Owners weighing a fixed-rate loan, equipment financing or a line-of-credit renewal may want to compare terms with a lender or advisor before that meeting.

There is some relief in the details. Grocery prices are barely moving, which helps restaurants and food businesses on the input side even as they raise menu prices modestly. Used vehicles are cheaper than a year ago, which matters for small fleets replacing trucks. And core goods and services outside energy are rising at a pace that, while above the Fed's target, is not running away.

Pricing decisions: when to pass costs through

The report suggests that many businesses are already passing energy costs through to customers. Airline fares are up more than 22% over the year, and restaurant prices continue to rise faster than grocery prices. For a small business, the practical question is whether a price increase is tied to a visible, explainable cost. Customers tend to accept fuel surcharges and delivery fees more readily when gas prices are in the headlines, and that window may narrow if pump prices keep easing.

Owners who sell on fixed-price contracts, such as contractors, landscapers and service firms quoting weeks in advance, face the most exposure. Building a short validity window into quotes, or indexing a fuel line item to a public benchmark like the EIA weekly average, can keep a profitable job from turning into a loss if diesel moves again.

What to watch next

The next data points arrive quickly. Producer prices and retail sales for September are scheduled for release on October 15, which will show whether wholesale costs are still climbing and whether consumers kept spending through the fuel shock. The Fed's Personal Consumption Expenditures inflation report, the central bank's preferred gauge, follows on October 29, the day after the rate decision.

  • October 15: Producer price index and retail sales for September.
  • October 20: EIA's next weekly gasoline and diesel price survey.
  • October 28: Federal Reserve rate decision.
  • November 10: October CPI, according to the BLS schedule.

For small business owners, the October 28 meeting is the date to circle. A second hike would not change the cost of fuel, but it would make every dollar borrowed to cover it more expensive.
