What changes for Colorado employers on January 1, 2027: wages, FAMLI premiums and the new state laws

The new year brings higher wage floors, a slightly lower paid-leave premium and a handful of 2026 laws that switch on for Colorado businesses. Here is what starts when, who it touches and what to do before the first 2027 payroll.

AGN Action Global News Staff Edited by Zach Wennstedt · Published November 17, 2026 · 9 min read
LinkedInXEmail

Key takeaways

  • Colorado's statewide minimum wage rises to $15.71 an hour on January 1, 2027, up 3.6% from $15.16; the tipped minimum rises to $12.69.
  • Denver's citywide minimum rises to $19.84 an hour on the same date. Employers generally must pay the highest rate that applies where the work is done.
  • The FAMLI paid-leave premium falls to 0.86% of wages in 2027, from 0.88% in 2026. Update payroll before the first 2027 pay run.
  • Colorado's rewritten AI law (SB26-189), agricultural overtime after 56 hours (SB26-121) and a workers' compensation rating fix (SB26-175) all take effect January 1, 2027.
  • Larger employers should calendar July 1, 2027, when state reporting of federal-style EEO-1 workforce data begins under HB26-1207.

Why does January 1 matter so much in Colorado?

Because Colorado ties several employer costs to the calendar year. The state minimum wage is reset for inflation each January, Denver adjusts its own higher wage floor on the same day, and the state's paid family and medical leave program, known as FAMLI, sets a new premium rate for each year. On top of that, several bills passed in the 2026 legislative session were written to take effect on January 1, 2027, giving businesses most of a year to prepare.

None of these changes is dramatic on its own. Together they touch payroll settings, posters, hiring software, insurance renewals and, for some owners, prices. This guide sorts the items by date and by the kind of business they affect.

How much is the minimum wage in 2027?

The Colorado Department of Labor and Employment announced on August 13, 2026, that the statewide minimum wage will be $15.71 an hour in 2027, up 3.6% from $15.16, according to ColoradoBiz. The minimum for tipped workers rises to $12.69 an hour. The adjustment is required by the state constitution and uses the Consumer Price Index for All Urban Consumers published by the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics.

The gap between the two rates reflects the state tip offset of $3.02, which The Colorado Sun reports is the statewide figure; local governments can set their own. An employer can count tips toward the wage only if the worker actually receives at least that amount in tips. If tips fall short in a pay period, the employer makes up the difference.

Denver and other local wage floors

Denver sets a separate, higher citywide minimum. It is $19.29 an hour in 2026 and rises to $19.84 on January 1, 2027, an increase of 55 cents, or about 2.85%, Denver7 reported. The city's Denver Labor minimum wage page explains that the Department of Finance adjusts the rate every January 1 using the Consumer Price Index, and that employers of tipped food and beverage workers may take a tip credit of up to $3.02 an hour. Denver7 put the matching tipped minimum at $16.82.

Denver is not alone. The Colorado Sun reported in August that Edgewater's council voted to match Denver's rate in 2027, and that Boulder County had planned a 3% increase that would bring its minimum to $17.32, with no final 2027 figure announced at the time. The City of Boulder has also paid above the state minimum since 2025. Because local rates can be set late in the year, check the city or county website for every location where your employees work, including staff who travel to job sites. ColoradoBiz notes that where state and local rules differ, employers generally must pay the higher rate.

One related point for contractors and service businesses with workers in several towns: Holland & Knight's summary of 2026 changes notes that SB26-193, effective on signing, confirmed that local minimum wage ordinances apply to private employers.

What happens to FAMLI premiums?

The FAMLI Division told employers in a September 30, 2026 bulletin that the 2027 premium rate will be 0.86% of wages, down from 0.88% in 2026, effective January 1, 2027. It also reminded employers that updating your payroll settings before your first 2027 pay run keeps deductions accurate from the start.

How the premium is shared depends on company size. According to a Paychex compliance guide, employers may split the premium with employees 50/50 or pay up to the whole amount, and employers with fewer than 10 employees are not required to pay the employer share, though they must still withhold and send in the employee share. Wage reports and premiums are filed quarterly through the state's My FAMLI+ Employer portal; the third-quarter 2026 filing was due October 31, according to the same state bulletin.

For a business with a $50,000 annual payroll, the drop from 0.88% to 0.86% is worth about $10 a year in total premium. The amount is small. The cost of getting the rate wrong in payroll software, and then correcting a year of deductions, is not.

Which 2026 laws switch on January 1, 2027?

The nonpartisan Legislative Council Staff publishes a summary of major labor and employment legislation from each session, and law firms have published their own reviews. Based on those summaries, these are the items with a January 1, 2027 start date that most small and midsize employers should know about.

Automated decision tools in hiring and HR (SB26-189)

SB26-189 replaced the state's 2024 artificial intelligence law with a narrower set of duties that apply when automated decision-making technology materially influences a consequential decision, such as screening resumes or scoring interviews. Holland & Knight's summary says employers must give applicants clear notice that such a tool will be used, send a follow-up notice within 30 days of an adverse outcome, offer a way to correct inaccurate personal data and, where commercially reasonable, provide meaningful human review. Records must be kept for at least three years. A JD Supra session summary adds that the attorney general enforces the law with a 60-day cure period for most first-time violations. We covered the law in detail in our SB26-189 explainer.

The practical first step for a small employer is an inventory: list every hiring, scheduling, pay and performance tool you use and ask each vendor whether it scores, ranks or filters people. Many businesses use these features without thinking of them as AI.

Agricultural overtime and penalties (SB26-121)

Starting January 1, 2027, certain agricultural employees are entitled to overtime for hours over 56 in a workweek, according to both the Legislative Council summary and the JD Supra review. Exemptions cover employees principally engaged in range production of livestock, qualifying decision-making managers, and family members of family-owned agricultural employers. The bill also raises penalties for agricultural employers that repeatedly fail to pay wages or misclassify workers. Farms, ranches and nurseries should check timekeeping now so that hours are recorded by workweek, not just by season.

Workers' compensation experience ratings (SB26-175)

This one can save money. For claims closed on or after January 1, 2027, the JD Supra summary says insurers must have the rating organization adjust an employer's experience modification factor based on the lower amount actually paid on a closed claim. The adjustment applies if it lowers the factor by at least 0.05, or brings it from above 1.0 to 1.0 or below, and the employer has to notify its carrier within a set window. If you had a large claim that closed for less than it was reserved, ask your agent about this at renewal.

Extreme temperature protections (HB26-1272)

HB26-1272 starts with data. The state labor department begins collecting information on temperature-related injuries and illnesses at work in early 2027, then publishes a model prevention plan that employers in covered industries will later have to adopt. The summaries differ on exact dates: Legislative Council Staff lists data collection by January 15, 2027 and the model plan by July 1, 2028, while the JD Supra review lists a January 1, 2027 start, a model plan by January 1, 2028 and employer plans due September 1, 2028. Outdoor and warehouse employers should watch for the state's guidance and check the enacted text.

What is already in effect that is easy to miss?

Several 2026 changes took effect on signing or in the summer, and they belong on the same checklist:

  • Voting leave (HB26-1113): Holland & Knight says employers of all sizes must give registered voters up to two hours of paid leave, once, on any day voter service and polling centers are open during an election, not only on Election Day.
  • Identity documents (HB26-1283): A review of final session outcomes reports that employers generally may not demand or keep workers' government-issued IDs, may hold one for Form I-9 purposes only as long as needed and no more than ten hours, and must give written notice of these protections in English and the worker's primary language, if known.
  • Workers' comp verification (SB26-093): For projects over $1 million, permitting agencies must confirm coverage for general contractors and subcontractors before issuing permits, according to the JD Supra review.
  • Posters and recordkeeping: Colorado's 2026 overtime and minimum pay rules, known as COMPS Order #40, took effect February 1, 2026, and require the current poster plus tracking of vacation and paid sick leave balances, Holland & Knight notes. Expect an updated poster for 2027 wage figures.

What comes later in 2027?

Private employers with about 100 or more workers should calendar July 1, 2027. Under HB26-1207, they must begin reporting workforce demographic data that mirrors the federal EEO-1 form, as it existed on March 1, 2026, to the Colorado Secretary of State as part of their periodic reports. The summaries differ slightly on whether the threshold is 100 workers or more than 100, so confirm against the bill. The requirement continues even if the federal EEO-1 report is discontinued.

Owners should also plan for personal tax changes. After voters approved Amendment 87, Colorado moves to six income tax brackets ranging from 3.7% to 8.4% starting with tax year 2027, as we reported. That matters to owners of pass-through businesses and to employees reviewing their state withholding.

A checklist before the first 2027 payroll

  1. Map every work location. List each city and county where employees work, including job sites, and look up the 2027 rate for each.
  2. Update base rates. Raise anyone below the new floor effective with work performed on or after January 1, and review the pay of people just above it.
  3. Check tipped pay. Confirm the cash wage and tip credit for each location and that tip records support the credit.
  4. Change the FAMLI rate to 0.86% in payroll software, and confirm how the premium is split between the business and employees.
  5. Inventory hiring and HR software for automated scoring or screening, and draft the notices SB26-189 requires.
  6. Replace posters when the state and local 2027 versions are published, and update handbooks that quote wage figures.
  7. Review contracts and prices that were set using 2026 labor costs.
  8. Ask your insurance agent about the workers' compensation rating adjustment for closed claims.
  9. Calendar July 1, 2027 for EEO-1 style reporting if you have about 100 or more workers.

This guide summarizes public announcements and legal summaries; it is not legal advice. For questions about a specific situation, the Colorado Department of Labor and Employment and local labor offices answer employer questions, and an employment attorney can review policies against the enacted text.

Frequently asked

What is Colorado's minimum wage in 2027?

The statewide minimum wage is $15.71 an hour starting January 1, 2027, up from $15.16. The tipped minimum is $12.69 an hour. Some cities and counties, including Denver, set higher local rates.

What is Denver's minimum wage in 2027?

Denver's citywide minimum wage rises to $19.84 an hour on January 1, 2027, from $19.29. Employers of tipped food and beverage workers may take a tip credit of up to $3.02 an hour.

What is the FAMLI premium rate for 2027?

The FAMLI Division says the 2027 premium is 0.86% of wages, down from 0.88% in 2026. Employers with 10 or more employees may split it 50/50 with workers; smaller employers do not have to pay the employer share but must still remit the employee share.

Which new Colorado employment laws start January 1, 2027?

Summaries of the 2026 session list SB26-189 on automated decision-making tools, SB26-121 on agricultural overtime after 56 hours, SB26-175 on workers' compensation experience ratings and the first steps of HB26-1272 on temperature-related illness. HB26-1207 workforce data reporting begins July 1, 2027.
