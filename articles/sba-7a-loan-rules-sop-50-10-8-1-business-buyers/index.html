Small Business · What it means

New SBA loan rules took effect Oct. 1. Buying a small business with a 7(a) loan now takes more cash, more proof and a citizenship check

The Small Business Administration's updated lending rulebook applies to every 7(a) and 504 loan numbered on or after October 1. The biggest changes land on people buying, expanding or buying out a business, and they are easier to plan around before a letter of intent than after.

AGN Action Global News Staff Small Business desk · Published October 15, 2026 · 6 min read
LinkedInXFacebookEmail

Key takeaways

  • SBA's SOP 50 10 8.1 took effect October 1, 2026, and applies to 7(a) and 504 applications that receive an SBA loan number on or after that date.
  • For most change-of-ownership loans, the required debt service coverage rises to 1.25x, measured on historical earnings rather than projections; business expansions use 1.15x.
  • Acquisitions and expansions priced at $3 million or more need a quality of earnings report, and every change of ownership needs an independent valuation.
  • Every owner and required guarantor must be a U.S. citizen or U.S. national whose principal residence is in the United States.

Buying an existing business has been one of the most common uses of the Small Business Administration's flagship 7(a) loan. As of October 1, it is also one of the most heavily rewritten. The agency's updated rulebook, Standard Operating Procedure 50 10 8.1, took effect that day and governs every 7(a) and 504 application that receives an SBA loan number on or after it, according to the SBA's information notice. Applications numbered through September 30 stay under the previous version.

The SBA released the new procedure on August 14 in an email from Thomas Kimsey, associate administrator of the Office of Capital Access, Coleman Report reported. Lenders had about seven weeks to adjust. Buyers, sellers and brokers working on deals now are the first to live with the result.

What changed for buying a business?

The new rules sort every change of ownership into one of four categories, set out in a new Appendix 15: an initial acquisition, a business expansion, an owner buyout, or an employee stock ownership plan or cooperative transaction, according to summaries from accounting firms Doeren Mayhew and PBMares. The category decides which tests apply. A business expansion, for example, generally means buying a company in the same six-digit NAICS industry code, by a buyer that has owned its current business for two full fiscal years and has positive net worth, PBMares wrote.

A higher cash-flow bar, measured on the past

Under the previous rules, a 1.15x debt service coverage ratio was the usual floor. For most change-of-ownership transactions it now rises to 1.25x, the law firm PilieroMazza wrote. In plain terms, the business being bought must have earned at least $1.25 in available cash flow for every $1 of annual debt payments. Expansions keep the 1.15x level.

Just as important is which earnings count. "Lenders can no longer use forecasted post-closing earnings" to meet the floor, PBMares wrote. A buyer who plans to cut costs or raise prices after closing cannot lean on that plan to qualify. The numbers have to be in the seller's history.

Quality of earnings reports and valuations

For initial acquisitions and expansions with a purchase price of $3 million or more, a quality of earnings report is now mandatory. Doeren Mayhew says it must include a "cash proof" covering the trailing 12 months and the last two fiscal years, reconciling bank activity to reported results, and the lender must keep it in the loan file. PilieroMazza adds that the lender's analysis includes a customer concentration review. Owner buyouts and ESOP deals are exempt from the requirement, according to PBMares.

Separately, every change of ownership now needs an independent valuation from a credentialed source, and the waiver that let lenders rely on an internal estimate is gone. If the agreed price is higher than the valuation, the difference must be covered with cash equity, not loan proceeds, PBMares wrote. Sellers holding out for a premium price may find buyers asking them to justify it against an appraisal earlier in negotiations.

The deal now has to work on the seller's past numbers, at a higher coverage ratio, with an independent valuation behind the price.

Small deals lose their shortcut

Loans of $350,000 or less used to qualify for streamlined underwriting. For acquisitions, that shortcut is gone. PilieroMazza wrote that all acquisitions are treated as standard 7(a) transactions regardless of size, with a full credit memo, an independent valuation, site visits and the historical cash-flow test. Doeren Mayhew also notes that the SBA's small-loan credit scoring approach has changed, and lenders must now include a repayment analysis using the two most recent months of bank statements.

Equity, seller notes and outside investors

Certain transactions, including business expansions and partner buyouts, require a minimum equity injection of 10%, according to PilieroMazza. Non-cash sources such as qualifying seller notes, third-party notes and standby debt together cannot exceed half of that minimum. PBMares describes a related cap: equity from investors who will own less than 20%, combined with seller standby debt, is limited to 50% of the required injection, where there was previously no cap on investor equity.

Owner buyouts carry their own guardrail. Outside investors cannot take a majority stake or become the largest shareholder without the deal being reclassified as a first-time acquisition, with the stricter tests that come with it, PBMares wrote. Lenders will also assess whether the buyer has enough liquidity to run the business after closing.

Who is eligible to own

The new SOP incorporates citizenship and residency rules that took effect earlier in 2026. SBA financing is limited to businesses whose direct and indirect owners and required guarantors are U.S. citizens or U.S. nationals with a principal residence in the United States, and any owning entity must be organized in the United States. Lawful permanent residents, who could previously own 100% of a borrower, are no longer eligible, according to PilieroMazza. One ineligible owner disqualifies the entire loan, PBMares noted. The fintech company Novo reported that the requirement has applied since March 1, 2026.

What got easier

Not every change tightens. Coleman Report noted that the SOP simplifies equity requirements for business acquisition and owner buyout loans, lets eligible change-of-ownership deals pair with revolving lines of credit through an expanded Manufacturers' Access to Revolving Credit program and a working capital pilot, gives lenders more flexibility to extend and reissue SBA Express loans, and allows more same-institution refinancing. The SBA said the revolving-line option "may reduce required monthly term-loan payments," according to Coleman Report. The agency is also revising its application forms; until they are released, lenders must collect the information the SOP requires and keep it in the file.

What buyers and sellers should do now

  1. Talk to an SBA lender before signing a letter of intent. PilieroMazza recommends settling the financing structure first, because coverage, valuation and equity rules now shape what price a lender can support.
  2. Pull three years of clean financials. Tax returns, bank statements and the trailing 12 months should reconcile. A quality of earnings provider will test exactly that on deals of $3 million or more.
  3. Run the 1.25x math on historical numbers. If the deal only works with post-closing improvements, expect to bring more cash or renegotiate the price.
  4. Price against an appraisal. Any premium above the independent valuation must be covered in cash.
  5. Confirm every owner's eligibility. Check citizenship and residency for all direct and indirect owners and guarantors early.
  6. Budget time. Valuations, site visits and earnings reports add steps, even on small deals.

The summaries cited here are from law and accounting firms that work with SBA lenders. The SOP 50 10 8.1 text on the SBA's website is the controlling document, and buyers should confirm details with their lender and advisors. Frequently asked

Which loans follow SOP 50 10 8.1?

SBA 7(a) and 504 applications that receive an SBA loan number on or after October 1, 2026. Applications numbered through September 30 stay under SOP 50 10 8.

When is a quality of earnings report required for an SBA loan?

Under SOP 50 10 8.1, initial acquisitions and business expansions with a purchase price of $3 million or more require a quality of earnings report. Owner buyouts and ESOP transactions are exempt, according to law and accounting firm summaries.

Can a green card holder still get an SBA 7(a) loan?

Not as an owner or required guarantor. The rules limit SBA financing to businesses whose direct and indirect owners and required guarantors are U.S. citizens or U.S. nationals with a principal residence in the United States.
