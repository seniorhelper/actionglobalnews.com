Europe's border check now takes your fingerprints. The travel authorization is still coming. What Americans need to know

Visitors from outside the EU are now registered with a face scan and fingerprints on their first trip into the Schengen area. The separate online travel authorization, ETIAS, has slipped again. Here is what applies now, what does not, and how to avoid the worst queues.

AGN Action Global News Staff Travel desk · Edited by the AGN news desk · Published October 10, 2026 · 5 min read
LinkedInXFacebookEmail

Key takeaways

  • The EU’s Entry/Exit System became fully operational on April 10, 2026, across 29 European countries, replacing passport stamps with digital records for short-stay visitors from outside the EU.
  • On a first entry, the system records a facial image, four fingerprints and passport data; children under 12 are photographed but not fingerprinted. Later trips need only a face check.
  • The early weeks of full operation brought long queues and missed flights at some airports, according to industry reports.
  • ETIAS, the online travel authorization that visa-exempt visitors such as Americans will eventually need, is not in operation. The EU removed its late-2026 target date and says it will announce the start months in advance.

Anyone who has flown to Paris, Rome or Frankfurt on an American passport since April has met the European Union’s Entry/Exit System, even if they did not know its name. The system, known as EES, became fully operational on April 10, 2026, across 29 European countries, according to an EY summary of the Commission’s announcement. It replaced the familiar ink stamp with a digital record tied to the traveler’s face and fingerprints.

A second change, the European Travel Information and Authorisation System, or ETIAS, has been talked about for years and is still not running. The two are often confused. They are separate systems, and only one of them affects travelers today.

What EES is

EES is an automated system that registers non-EU nationals traveling for short stays each time they cross an external border of the participating countries. It applies to people who need a short-stay visa and to those who do not, such as U.S. citizens, for stays of up to 90 days in any 180-day period, as Lufthansa Group’s explainer describes it.

The countries involved are the EU members that use the system plus Iceland, Liechtenstein, Norway and Switzerland. Ireland and Cyprus do not apply it.

The rollout was phased over six months starting in October 2025, with border points switching on gradually. That transitional period ended in April, and since then all participating countries have been required to use the system at every border crossing point.

Not every non-EU national is registered. People who hold a residence permit or long-stay visa issued by a participating country are generally outside the system, because EES is designed for short stays. British citizens, who became third-country nationals when the United Kingdom left the EU, are covered on the same basis as Americans.

What happens at the border

On your first entry under EES, border authorities record a facial image, fingerprints of four fingers and the data from your passport. On later trips, a facial check is usually enough. The data is kept for three years. Children under 12 are photographed but not fingerprinted.

Many airports use self-service kiosks where travelers with biometric passports scan their own documents and fingerprints before seeing an officer. A machine-readable biometric passport, which all current U.S. passports are, is a prerequisite for the kiosks.

Two rules about where the check happens matter for connecting flights. Entry is recorded at the first airport where you arrive in the Schengen area, even if you are connecting onward. Exit is recorded at the last Schengen airport before you leave. A traveler flying from Denver to Rome through Frankfurt therefore registers in Frankfurt.

The stamp used to be the record of your stay. Now the record is you, and it counts your days for you.

The 90/180 rule is now enforced by software

The rule itself has not changed: visa-exempt visitors may stay up to 90 days in any rolling 180-day period across the whole Schengen area. What has changed is that EES calculates it automatically from recorded entries and exits, instead of relying on officers reading stamps. Overstays are flagged by the system.

For most tourists this makes no difference. For frequent business travelers, digital nomads and people who split time between the United States and Europe, it removes any room for error. Keep your own record of entries and exits and count backward 180 days from any planned date before booking.

The queues

The first weeks of full operation were rough at some airports. EY reported substantial delays at major airports, including missed flights and extended queues at border control. Travel Radar, citing BBC reporting, described waits of two to three hours or more at some passport halls in April. Registration takes longer than a stamp, particularly for first-time travelers who must give fingerprints.

Practical steps help:

  • Allow more connection time when your first Schengen airport is also where you change planes.
  • Expect the first entry to take longest. Repeat visits within three years should be faster.
  • Keep passports handy for the whole family, including children, who must also be registered.
  • Use kiosks where offered, but expect to see an officer afterward.

ETIAS: still on the way

ETIAS will require visa-exempt travelers, including Americans, to apply online and pay a fee before traveling to most of Europe. The EU had most recently targeted the last quarter of 2026. That target has been dropped. The official ETIAS website now says the system is currently not in operation and that no applications are being collected, and iVisa reported in July that the EU had removed the 2026 launch date without a formal announcement.

An eu-LISA spokesperson, quoted in travel-industry coverage, acknowledged that launching ETIAS before the end of this year is no longer realistic. Commentators widely expect a launch in 2027, but no date has been confirmed, and only a formal decision by the European Commission sets it. The EU has said it will announce the start date several months before launch.

Until then, there is nothing to apply for. Websites offering to process ETIAS applications today are not official. When ETIAS does open, the official application will be through the EU’s own website and app.

  • Check your passport
