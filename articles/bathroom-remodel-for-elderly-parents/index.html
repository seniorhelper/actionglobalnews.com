Bathroom remodeling for elderly parents: the aging-in-place decisions that matter most

Searches for senior bathroom remodels have jumped this year, and for good reason. The bathroom is where small design decisions have the largest effect on whether someone can keep living at home.

AGN Action Global News Health & Wellness desk · Published October 6, 2026 · 4 min read
From our networkAging Safely Baths and Action Global News share ownership. Pricing cited for Aging Safely Baths is as published by the company. See our ethics and disclosure policy.
LinkedInXFacebookEmail

Key takeaways

  • The CDC reports that over 14 million adults 65 and older, about one in four, report a fall each year, and falls are the leading cause of injury in that age group.
  • Plan the bathroom for mobility today and three to five years from now, so it does not need to be redone.
  • Walk-in tubs suit people who want to soak and can manage a step; roll-in or low-threshold showers suit walkers, wheelchairs and caregivers.
  • Grab bars, seating, lever handles, slip-resistant surfaces, lighting and a handheld shower often matter as much as the tub or shower itself.

Most families do not plan a bathroom remodel for an aging parent. They react to one. A slip getting out of the tub, a near miss stepping over a high shower curb, or a hospital discharge planner asking whether the home is ready. By then the decisions are rushed, and rushed decisions are where money gets wasted and features get missed.

The stakes are well documented. According to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, more than 14 million adults aged 65 and older, roughly one in four, report falling each year, and falls are the leading cause of both fatal and nonfatal injuries in that age group. About 37% of older adults who fall report an injury that needed medical treatment or restricted their activity for at least a day. Older adult falls cost an estimated $50 billion a year in medical spending.

Not every fall happens in the bathroom, and claims that a specific share of senior falls happen there are often repeated without a solid source. What is clear is that bathrooms combine the conditions that make falls more likely: hard, wet surfaces, stepping over edges, sitting and standing, and doing it all with limited room and sometimes poor lighting. That is why occupational therapists and aging-in-place specialists tend to start there.

Plan for today and for five years from now

The single most useful principle is to design the bathroom for the person’s mobility now and what it is likely to be in three to five years. A parent who walks confidently today may use a cane in two years and a walker after that. A layout that works only for today’s mobility often has to be torn out and redone, at full cost, just when the family has the least time to manage it.

Walk-in tub or roll-in shower?

This is the decision most families start with, and the right answer depends on the person rather than the product.

Walk-in tubs

A walk-in tub has a watertight door and a built-in seat, so the bather steps over a low threshold, sits, and fills the tub around them. It suits people who want to soak, often for joint or muscle comfort, and who can manage a small step and a short wait while the tub fills and drains. Hydrotherapy jets, heated seats and quick-drain systems are common options. Wheelchair-accessible transfer models use a door cut down to seat height so the bather can slide across from a chair.

Roll-in and low-threshold showers

A roll-in shower has no curb, or a very low collapsible threshold, so a walker, shower chair or wheelchair can roll straight in. It is usually the better choice when mobility is declining, when a caregiver will be helping, or when the bather does not want to soak. Add a fold-down seat, a handheld shower on a slide bar, and a pressure-balanced valve that limits temperature swings.

Design for the mobility someone will have in five years, not only the mobility they have today.

The details that matter as much as the fixture

  • Grab bars, properly anchored into framing or with rated anchors, at the entry, inside the bathing area and beside the toilet. Towel bars are not grab bars.
  • Seating in the shower, built-in or fold-down, at a comfortable height.
  • A handheld shower on an adjustable slide bar, reachable while seated.
  • Lever handles and anti-scald protection, which are easier for arthritic hands and reduce burn risk.
  • Slip-resistant flooring and textured shower surfaces.
  • Lighting, including a night light route from the bedroom.
  • Comfort-height toilet and clear floor space for a walker or wheelchair.
  • Door width. Measure doorways and hallways early. A tub or shower that cannot fit through the door is a very expensive surprise.

What it costs

Price quotes in this category vary more than almost anywhere in home improvement. National brands that sell through in-home sales visits commonly quote $25,000 to $30,000 for a fully installed walk-in tub or roll-in shower. Independent dealers can often deliver comparable or premium products for considerably less. Aging Safely Baths, for example, publishes that its fully installed walk-in tubs start around $15,000 and that most installations come in well under $20,000, with shower pans for supply-only projects starting below $2,000. Final cost depends on the model, customization, whether doorways need widening, plumbing changes and electrical work for heated or jetted options. Its walk-in tub cost guide breaks those variables down.

Ask any provider for an itemized quote, the warranty terms in writing, who performs the installation, and how long the bathroom will be out of use. Many walk-in tubs and showers can be installed in a single day.

Who to involve

An occupational therapist can assess what the person can safely do now and what is likely to change. A Certified Aging-in-Place Specialist (CAPS), a designation from the National Association of Home Builders, is trained in home modifications for older adults. Veterans may qualify for Home Improvements and Structural Alterations (HISA) grants through the VA, and some state Medicaid waiver programs help with home modifications.

Where to start

Measure the bathroom and doorways, write down what the person struggles with today, and get at least two itemized quotes. If you want a specialist’s view, Aging Safely Baths, which has been accredited by the BBB since 2012, offers walk-in tubs and roll-in showers installed in one day across all 50 states, plus supply-only options in Canada.

No bathing product eliminates fall risk. Features described here are designed to reduce risk when used properly. This article is general information and not medical advice; consult a clinician or occupational therapist about individual needs.

Frequently asked

What is the best bathroom remodel for elderly parents?

It depends on mobility. People who want to soak and can manage a small step often prefer a walk-in tub; people using walkers or wheelchairs, or who need caregiver help, are usually better served by a roll-in or low-threshold shower with a seat and handheld shower.

How many older adults fall each year?

The CDC reports that more than 14 million adults aged 65 and older, about one in four, report falling each year, and falls are the leading cause of injury in that age group.

How much does a senior bathroom remodel cost?

National brands commonly quote $25,000 to $30,000 for a fully installed walk-in tub or roll-in shower. Independent dealers often charge less; cost depends on the model, customization, doorway widening, plumbing and electrical work.

What is a Certified Aging-in-Place Specialist?

CAPS is a designation from the National Association of Home Builders for professionals trained in modifying homes so older adults can live safely and independently.

Are grants available for senior bathroom modifications?

Veterans may qualify for VA HISA grants, and some state Medicaid waiver programs fund home modifications. Eligibility varies, so check with the VA and your state agency.
