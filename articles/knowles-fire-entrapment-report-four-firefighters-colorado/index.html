A 102-page federal report says understaffed dispatch and a two-hour gap in air oversight preceded the Colorado burnover that killed four firefighters

Investigators found no single failure behind the June 27 deaths west of Grand Junction. They describe an overloaded dispatch center, crowded radio traffic and crews who wrongly believed an aircraft was watching the fire.

AGN Action Global News Staff Edited by Zach Wennstedt · Published November 13, 2026 · 2 min read
LinkedInXEmail

Key takeaways

  • The Interior Department released its 102-page investigation on November 12 into the June 27, 2026 entrapment near the Colorado-Utah border.
  • It cites strained communications, extreme fire behavior, understaffing and a gap in air oversight from about 2:30 to 4:30 p.m.
  • Four members of the Rifle Helitack crew died. The family of one is asking for dispatch, aviation and command records.

A federal investigation into the wildfire burnover that killed four firefighters in western Colorado in June found that strained communications, extreme fire behavior, understaffing and gaps in aerial supervision all played a part, The Colorado Sun reported. The U.S. Department of the Interior released the 102-page report on Thursday, November 12.

"No single decision or system failure fully explains the outcome," investigators wrote, according to the Sun.

What happened on June 27?

Members of the interagency Rifle Helitack crew were making an initial attack on lightning-caused fires near the Colorado-Utah border, west of Grand Junction, when they were overrun, according to the Wildland Fire Lessons Learned Center, which posted the report. Emily Barker, Nick Hutcherson and Sydney Watson died in the entrapment. Nathan Matthews died of his injuries on July 24.

The Sun reported that the Knowles fire was overtaken by the larger Snyder fire. A helicopter was sent at 3:08 p.m. but heavy smoke kept it from landing at 3:19 p.m., and the firefighters deployed their fire shelters. Temperatures near the shelters topped 1,200 degrees Fahrenheit, the report found.

What did investigators find?

The Grand Junction Interagency Dispatch Center was chronically understaffed, had a vacant manager position and relied on a high share of trainees, according to the Sun's account of the report. Dispatchers were tracking as many as 16 aircraft and three emerging incidents. Radio traffic was so crowded that a scheduled fire weather broadcast was never sent.

Turbulence and crosswinds of 40 to 50 mph left a gap in air attack coverage from about 2:30 to 4:30 p.m. Ground crews, helitack leaders and a pilot wrongly believed an aerial crew was watching the main fire, the Sun reported.

What happens next

The report points to areas for the new U.S. Wildland Fire Service to improve, including communications, real-time fire observation, airspace awareness and dispatch coordination. Hutcherson's parents are asking for dispatch and aviation records, aircraft tracks and records of command decisions, the Sun reported. The full report is available on the Lessons Learned Center's incident page.
