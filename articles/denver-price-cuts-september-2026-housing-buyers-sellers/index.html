Nearly one in three Denver listings cut its price in September, the second-highest share among big metros. What the record price cuts mean for buyers and sellers

Nationally, 20.8% of homes for sale had a price reduction in September, the highest share for the month in Realtor.com's records. Denver's share was 32.1%, behind only Salt Lake City. Inventory is close to pre-pandemic levels, but higher mortgage rates are limiting how many buyers can take advantage.

AGN Action Global News Staff Edited by Zach Wennstedt · Real Estate desk · Published October 15, 2026 · 5 min read
LinkedInXFacebookEmail

Key takeaways

  • 20.8% of U.S. listings had a price cut in September, up 0.9 percentage point from a year earlier and the highest September share on record, according to Realtor.com.
  • Denver's price-cut share was 32.1%, second among the 50 largest metros after Salt Lake City, and its median list price fell 4.9% from a year earlier to $569,900.
  • National active listings rose 5.4% to about 1.16 million, leaving inventory 9.1% below pre-pandemic levels, the smallest gap of the recovery.
  • Pending sales fell 4.1% from a year earlier as mortgage rates rose; Realtor.com's chief economist says rates are limiting how much buyers can use their new leverage.

Sellers across the country are cutting prices at a pace not seen in years, and few more so than in Denver. In September, 32.1% of homes for sale in the Denver metro had a price reduction, up 1.3 percentage points from a year earlier, according to Realtor.com's September housing report. Only Salt Lake City, at 33.6%, had a higher share among the 50 largest metros. Portland, Oregon, was third at 31.6%.

Nationally, 20.8% of listings had a price cut, up 0.9 percentage point from a year earlier and the highest September reading on record. "More owners are acknowledging that today's buyers need a lower price," said Jake Krimmel, a senior economist at Realtor.com.

What the September numbers show

Supply has rebuilt steadily. Active listings rose 5.4% from a year earlier to 1,161,615, and the gap with typical pre-pandemic inventory narrowed to 9.1%, the first time it has been below 10% in the current recovery, Realtor.com said. "The inventory gap with the pre-pandemic market is closing," Krimmel said.

Prices are giving way at the margins. The national median list price was $419,250, down 1.4% from a year earlier and 1.2% from August, the 11th straight month of annual declines, RISMedia reported. Price per square foot, which adjusts for the mix of homes on the market, fell 1.7%. Homes spent a median 61 days on the market.

The regional pattern favors buyers in the West. The West had the highest share of price cuts at 22.8%, followed by the South at 21.6% and the Midwest at 20.7%, with the Northeast lowest at 15.2%. Inventory grew fastest in the Northeast and Midwest, up more than 11% in each. The share of large metros where price cuts were more common than a year earlier jumped to 36 of 50, from 27 in August.

Why buyers are not rushing in

More choice and softer prices would normally pull buyers off the sidelines. Higher borrowing costs are working against that. Pending home sales, meaning homes under contract, fell 4.1% from a year earlier, the second straight monthly decline and the steepest drop since March 2025. Mortgage rates rose nearly 40 basis points over the four weeks before the report and were more than 70 basis points above a year earlier, Realtor.com said. Our report on mortgage rates and pending home sales covers how high rates have climbed since.

"Buyers are gaining leverage, but higher mortgage rates are limiting how much of that opportunity they can use," said Danielle Hale, Realtor.com's chief economist.

Data from the analytics firm HouseCanary points the same way. In single-family detached homes, contracts fell 10.8% from a year earlier in September, inventory was up 10.6%, and the market held 5.46 months of supply. Homes that did sell still closed at 97.7% of list price on average, and the median closed price was up 3.9% from a year earlier, which suggests well-priced homes are still finding buyers. Condos are softer, with 7.06 months of supply and a median list price down 3.7%. HouseCanary called the market "still in balance but under pressure" and warned that if rates stay elevated, this year's gains in supply, buyer activity and prices could reverse.

A price cut is not a sign that the market is collapsing. It is a sign that the first asking price was set for a market that no longer exists.

What it means in Denver

Denver's numbers describe a market that has already adjusted more than most. The metro's median list price of $569,900 is down 4.9% from a year earlier, and price per square foot is down 3.1%. New listings fell 5.9%, so the increase in inventory reflects homes sitting longer rather than a wave of new sellers.

Local agents have described the shift for months. In its June report, the Denver Metro Association of Realtors said buyers held "a slight edge over sellers" and were placing more weight on a home's condition. "This 'turnkey premium' is reshaping how both buyers and sellers think about value," Amanda Snitker, chair of the association's market trends committee, said at the time, according to ColoradoBiz.

What buyers, sellers and agents can do

For buyers:

  • Look at days on market and price history. A listing that has already been cut once is a signal the seller is open to negotiating.
  • Negotiate on more than price. Ask whether the seller will contribute to closing costs or a mortgage rate buydown, and compare the effect on your monthly payment with a lower price.
  • Get pre-approved and stress-test the payment at today's rates, not at the rate you hope for.

For sellers:

  • Price to the market from day one. With a third of Denver listings already reduced, buyers compare your price against recent cuts, not last spring's comparables.
  • Fix the obvious. Denver buyers have been paying a premium for move-in-ready homes, according to the association's committee.
  • Consider concessions before a second price cut, especially for buyers stretched by rates.

For agents, lenders and home-service businesses: with homes sitting longer and buyers favoring move-in-ready properties, sellers have more reason to invest in pre-sale repairs and presentation, and buyers have more room to ask for concessions. Pricing advice grounded in current reductions, rather than last year's sales, is likely to be the most valuable thing an agent offers this fall.
